How the Prediction Works

A week is drawn whole. To play one matchup, the model picks a week each team actually had and scores its twelve categories against the other's. Drawing the whole week matters: a team's ERA, WHIP and K/9 all move together with how its starters went, and a model that drew each category on its own would happily deal a team its best ERA of the year alongside its fewest strikeouts — a week that has never happened and never will.

The innings rule is simulated, not smoothed over. A drawn week under 50 IP forfeits all five pitching categories, exactly as Yahoo scores it. Managers who habitually run short carry that risk into their odds rather than getting credit for tidy rate stats posted on 35 innings.

Ties go to the better seed, which is the league's rule. A 6–6 week is not rare here, so it is worth knowing which way it falls before it happens.

Two prices, American moneyline. Negative is the favourite — −150 means risk 150 to win 100; positive is the dog — +200 means 100 returns 200. Fair is the model's probability converted straight across, with no margin: that is the break-even price, and nobody would book it. Vegas is what a house could actually post — the same probabilities with a margin added and rounded to a number that belongs on a board.

Vegas always pays you less than fair, and that is the juice. A smaller number is a worse deal, not a cheaper one. Josh at fair +172 returns 172 on a 100 stake; the Vegas +140 returns 140 for the same risk on the same outcome. Every price on the board is shortened the same way, which is why the fair column's implied chances add to exactly 100% while the Vegas column adds to more — the excess is the house edge, and it is printed in the column header.

A matchup is a three-way market — win, tie, loss — priced at a 5% hold, with the tie quoted next to its probability under the bar. A 6–6 week is a real result here, so pricing it two-way and calling ties a push would be a different bet from the one the model predicts. The title is a six-runner futures book at 20%, which is on the friendly side of what real books charge on futures.

The margin is loaded onto the longshots, not spread evenly. Multiplying every probability by the same factor would tax a 3% shot and a 60% favourite equally, which no book does; the prices here use the power method, so the outsiders give up proportionally more — the favourite-longshot bias that real boards have.

What it does not know. Injuries, call-ups, who is actually setting a lineup in September, and the two-start weeks that decide most pitching categories. A two-week window is eleven weeks of noise short of meaning anything on its own, which is why it is shown next to the season rather than instead of it.
